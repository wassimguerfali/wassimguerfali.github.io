Research

Does Allocation Mechanism Design Predict Road Quality? Evidence from Michigan’s Act 51

In progress Senior thesis · Advisor: Dr. Matthew Hinkel

Michigan distributes roughly two billion dollars of transportation revenue each year through Act 51, a statutory formula keyed to population, vehicle registrations, and road mileage rather than to road condition or use. I test whether the money that formula sends to a county actually shows up as better pavement, using a segment-level first-difference design: the outcome is the change in International Roughness Index on the same road segments across Michigan's biennial condition surveys, and the treatment is cumulative Act 51 funding per county lane-mile, lagged to each window. Differencing removes time-invariant segment and county characteristics and enforces the right temporal order — funding before the condition change it is meant to explain. Because condition is not an input to the allocation formula, the amount a county receives is plausibly independent of the state of its roads. A weak funding–condition link would imply that how Act 51 distributes money matters more than how much it distributes.

Data and method
  • Funding. Monthly Act 51 County Continuation Sheets from MDOT, parsed with a purpose-built Python pipeline (pdfplumber, Tesseract OCR, pandas) into a county-month panel of 2,988 county-months, reconciled against printed county totals.
  • Outcome. Two-year change in segment-level International Roughness Index, from the MDOT/TAMC pavement condition layer. Michigan surveys road classes in alternating years, so the sample pools two non-overlapping windows: 2023→2025 for principal arterials, 2022→2024 for minor arterials and major collectors.
  • Coverage. The condition program rates only federal-aid-eligible county roads, so the analysis covers arterials and collectors in roughly 55–60 of Michigan's 83 counties. Purely local roads are unrated and fall outside the measured outcome.
  • Controls. Baseline roughness, segment-level total and commercial (truck) AADT from ~24,500 spatially joined traffic segments (24,499 matched), functional class, lanes, segment length, and the county characteristics that drive the allocation formula.
  • Estimation. OLS with survey-window fixed effects. Funding varies only at the county level, so standard errors are clustered by county and corroborated with wild cluster bootstrap given the small cluster count. A fractional specification on the share rated Poor is reported as a robustness check.
  • Context. A 24-source literature review situating the work in the public finance and infrastructure investment literature.

Union Contractors, Construction Cost, and Project Delivery in Santa Clara County

Co-authored — data collection with Dr. Matthew Hinkel (Alma College) and Larissa Petrucci (NCIC)

An extension of earlier Sacramento County work asking how contractor signatory status relates to public construction outcomes: total cost, schedule performance, and the composition of the workforce on site. I own the certified payroll data workstream — turning thousands of pages of electronic certified payroll records into project-level measures of unique workers by craft, apprentice hours as a share of total hours, and union density across primes and subcontractors. Intended for submission to a peer-reviewed journal.

My role
Designed the extraction schema separating per-project worker rosters from the project-level aggregates used in analysis, and built and benchmarked the pipeline that produces them. Underlying payroll records are confidential; no data or findings are posted here.
